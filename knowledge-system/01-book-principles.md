# D. Principles extracted from each book (Phase 1)

Evidence tags: **[R]** research cited by the author · **[F]** author or field framework · **[A]** anecdote only · **[GK]** general-knowledge caveat, not from the sources. All examples and exercises are original. Book wording is paraphrased; the only quotations are short technique names and phrases the techniques depend on.

Each concept card covers the 14 required fields in compact form:
**Category · Description · Why · Use when / Not when · Observable behavior · Mistake → Better · Scenario · Exercise · Evaluation · Source**

## 1. *Never Split the Difference* (Voss & Raz): NSTD

### 1.1 Book-level analysis

| Dimension | Findings |
|---|---|
| **Core ideas** | People decide emotionally first and then rationalize (System 1 / System 2, Ch.1). Negotiation is *discovery*, not argument. The listener controls the conversation. Make the other side feel safe and in control, so they talk and end up proposing your solution. "Yes" without "How" is worthless. |
| **Communication principles** | Slow down. Concentrate fully on the other person, not your own inner dialogue. Use deliberate voice tones: calm downward "late-night FM DJ" for key points, positive/playful as the default, assertive rarely (Ch.2). Use silence. |
| **Sales principles** (implied) | Uncover what the counterpart is worried about and what they hope for, not just their stated position (Appendix). Status quo has a cost ("What does doing nothing cost you?"). Find the decision makers behind the table (Ch.7–8). Re-engage silent prospects with a no-oriented message (Ch.4). |
| **Negotiation principles** | Don't compromise for its own sake. No deal is better than a bad deal (Ch.6). Usually let the other side anchor money first. If you go first, use a range (Ch.6). Pivot to non-monetary terms. Use the Ackerman offer plan (Ch.9). Don't be needy, and be ready to walk away (Ch.9). |
| **Persuasion principles** | Loss aversion: show what they lose by not acting (Ch.6, citing Kahneman/Tversky). Get them to feel the idea is theirs (Ch.4/7). "Fair" is an emotional trigger (Ch.6). |
| **Psychological principles** | Naming an emotion reduces its intensity [R, citing Lieberman et al. 2007]. Similarity creates bonding (mirroring) [R, Wiseman waiter study as reported]. Unconditional positive regard (Carl Rogers) leads to behavior change (Ch.5). Fear of loss weighs more than equivalent gain [R]. |
| **Practical techniques** | Mirror, label, accusation audit, effective pauses, minimal encouragers, paraphrase, summary → "that's right", no-oriented questions, calibrated How/What questions, "How am I supposed to do that?", Rule of Three, Ackerman model, 4 ways to say no, negotiation one sheet. |
| **Common mistakes** | Rushing. Treating assumptions as facts. Pushing for "yes". Accepting "you're right" as agreement. Asking "why" (sounds accusatory). Splitting the difference. Aiming low because you fixate on your walk-away (BATNA). Ignoring people behind the table. Treating the counterpart as the enemy. |
| **Behaviors to develop** | Curiosity, labeling emotions out loud, comfort with silence and with "no", calm tone, preparing labels and questions in advance, testing whether an agreement will actually be carried out. |
| **Behaviors to avoid** | Arguing with logic. Aggressive tone. Answering a punch with anger. Needing the deal. Unchecked deadline pressure. |
| **Exercises (stated/implied)** | Practice mirrors and labels in everyday conversations "even though they feel awkward" (Ch.3). Write a one sheet before each negotiation (Appendix). Roleplay the accusation audit (Appendix). Try "why" on people and watch them get defensive (Ch.9). |
| **Real-world situations** | Salary negotiation, car buying, collecting unpaid invoices, clients asking for extra work, landlord negotiations, vendor pricing, family disputes. All examples in the book. |

### 1.2 Concept cards

#### NSTD-1 · Negotiation as discovery (hypotheses, not assumptions)
- **Category:** Discovery / mindset · [F]
- **Description:** Go into a call with several hypotheses about the other side's needs and use the conversation to test them. Expect to be surprised, and look for the surprises on purpose.
- **Why:** Fixed assumptions make you pitch the wrong thing and miss hidden deal-breakers.
- **Use when:** Any first or discovery call. **Not when:** The facts are already confirmed in writing and the call is pure admin.
- **Observable behavior:** States or tests assumptions as questions ("My guess is X. How close is that?"). Changes direction when new information comes in.
- **Mistake → Better:** Opening with a pitch built on assumed pain → open by checking hypotheses ("Most clinics I talk to are fighting no-shows. Is that even on your radar?").
- **Scenario:** A web developer assumes a restaurant wants a new website. Discovery shows the owner's real pain is phone orders blocking the line at peak hours.
- **Exercise:** Before a roleplay, write 3 hypotheses. Afterwards, mark each one confirmed, refuted or untested.
- **Evaluation:** At least 2 hypotheses tested by questions. The pitch changes after a refuted hypothesis.
- **Source:** NSTD Ch.2 (Key Lessons), Ch.10.

#### NSTD-2 · Voice tones (late-night FM DJ / playful / assertive)
- **Category:** Communication fundamentals (tone) · [F]
- **Description:** Three voice modes. Playful/positive is the default. Calm, slow, downward inflection marks non-negotiable points. Assertive is used rarely because it creates pushback.
- **Why:** Tone signals safety or threat before the words register. Smiling while speaking makes people think more flexibly (author's claim).
- **Use when:** DJ tone for a price, a boundary or bad news. Playful tone to build rapport. **Not when:** Assertive tone should not be used on someone who is already defensive.
- **Observable behavior:** Voice mode: pace slows on key statements, statements end with downward inflection. Text mode (proxy): short declarative sentences for boundaries, no exclamation-heavy pressure, softeners ("I'm afraid…").
- **Mistake → Better:** Stating your price with a rising, uncertain tone or an over-explaining paragraph → one calm sentence, then a pause.
- **Scenario:** A freelancer states their day rate and stops talking.
- **Exercise:** Say your price 5 times in DJ voice and record it. The coach rates pace and whether you kept justifying it.
- **Evaluation:** Price or boundary delivered in ≤2 sentences with no justification spiral. Voice mode: words per minute drops on the key line.
- **Source:** NSTD Ch.2.

#### NSTD-3 · Mirroring
- **Category:** Active listening · [F] + [R as reported]
- **Description:** Repeat the last 1–3 key words the other person said, with a curious tone, then stay silent.
- **Why:** It suggests similarity, gets them to expand, and buys you time. Voss reports a waiter study in which mirroring raised tips.
- **Use when:** They say something vague or important ("We had a bad experience…" → "A bad experience?"). **Not when:** Used over and over in a row, which starts to sound robotic or like mockery.
- **Observable behavior:** Repeats 1–3 of the counterpart's own words as a question, then waits.
- **Mistake → Better:** Mirroring then adding your own point right away → mirror, then silence.
- **Scenario:** Prospect: "Honestly the last agency burned us." User: "Burned you?"
- **Exercise:** A 5-minute roleplay where the user may *only* mirror, label or stay silent. Count how much new information appears.
- **Evaluation:** At least 2 mirrors in a discovery call, each followed by the prospect elaborating. No more than 2 mirrors in a row.
- **Source:** NSTD Ch.2.

#### NSTD-4 · Labeling
- **Category:** Empathy · [F] + [R: affect labeling]
- **Description:** Name the other person's emotion or position as an observation: "It seems like…", "It sounds like…", "It looks like…". Then pause.
- **Why:** Naming a negative emotion reduces it. Naming a positive one reinforces it. People usually correct or expand on a label, which gives you information.
- **Use when:** Emotion, hesitation, a barrier, or a strong position appears. **Not when:** Starting with "I'm hearing that…". Voss says to avoid "I", because it makes the conversation about you.
- **Observable behavior:** Statements starting "It seems/sounds/looks like…" that refer to *their* feelings or constraints.
- **Mistake → Better:** "I understand how you feel" (a claim) → "It sounds like you're worried this will be another project that drags on" (evidence that you listened).
- **Scenario:** Prospect hesitates on price → "It seems like you've been burned paying upfront before."
- **Exercise:** For 10 written objections, write one label each. Then label live in roleplay.
- **Evaluation:** At least 1 label per emotional moment. Label is specific (mentions their content). No "I" opener.
- **Source:** NSTD Ch.3; Appendix (fill-in labels).

#### NSTD-5 · Accusation audit
- **Category:** Objection prevention / trust · [F]
- **Description:** List the worst things the other person might think about you or your offer, and say them first, calmly, before they can.
- **Why:** It takes the force out of negative feelings, and when you say an exaggerated version, people often argue the opposite.
- **Use when:** Bad news, a high price, a cold call, a known reputation problem. **Not when:** Inventing problems that aren't there, or self-deprecating so much that you lose credibility.
- **Observable behavior:** Early statements like "You're probably thinking this is another agency that overpromises…" followed by a pause.
- **Mistake → Better:** Saving the price shock for the end → "You're going to think this is expensive for a 5-page site…"
- **Scenario:** A freelancer has to announce a delay to a client.
- **Exercise:** Before each scenario, write 3–5 likely accusations. The coach checks whether they were used.
- **Evaluation:** Audit appears before the ask or bad news. Each item is specific. A pause follows.
- **Source:** NSTD Ch.3; Ch.6 (anchoring emotions); Appendix §III.

#### NSTD-6 · Active-listening toolkit: pauses, minimal encouragers, paraphrase, summary
- **Category:** Active listening · [F] (FBI Crisis Negotiation Unit)
- **Description:** Effective pauses (silence), minimal encouragers ("OK", "I see"), paraphrasing (their meaning in your words), and **summary = paraphrase + label**.
- **Why:** These are the first step of the Behavioral Change Stairway. Influence isn't possible until the other person feels understood.
- **Use when:** Throughout discovery, and always before you make a recommendation. **Not when:** Encouragers stand in for real engagement (Lowndes makes a parallel warning: use full "empathizers", not just "uh-huh").
- **Observable behavior:** Summaries that capture their situation and their feelings. Turn-taking where the prospect talks more than the user.
- **Mistake → Better:** Jumping from their answer straight to your pitch → summarize first ("So you're… and it sounds like… Did I get that right?").
- **Scenario:** End of discovery: "So the site loads slowly, mobile orders are down, and it sounds like you feel stuck because your nephew built it."
- **Exercise:** After each prospect turn, the user writes a one-line summary. The coach compares it to the prospect's hidden state.
- **Evaluation:** At least 1 full summary before any recommendation. Prospect's talk-time ≥ 50% in discovery scenarios.
- **Source:** NSTD Ch.5.

#### NSTD-7 · "That's right" over "You're right"
- **Category:** Rapport / commitment signal · [F]
- **Description:** Aim for the prospect saying "that's right" in response to your summary of their world. "You're right" usually means they want you to stop talking.
- **Why:** "That's right" means they feel understood and own the conclusion. "You're right" is a polite brush-off with no commitment.
- **Use when:** As the checkpoint before you propose anything. **Not when:** Fishing for "that's right" with leading questions.
- **Observable behavior:** In the roleplay, the AI prospect produces "that's right" (or "exactly") only when the summary matches its hidden state.
- **Mistake → Better:** Treating "you're right" as agreement and moving to close → treat it as a warning, re-summarize, label any hesitation.
- **Scenario:** An upsell call where the client says "you're right, we should" but changes nothing.
- **Exercise:** A "That's-right hunt": the user gets 3 tries to summarize until the AI confirms.
- **Evaluation:** Earned "that's right" before the proposal = strong signal. Pushing ahead after "you're right" = flagged.
- **Source:** NSTD Ch.5.

#### NSTD-8 · No-oriented questions
- **Category:** Discovery / closing / follow-up · [F]
- **Description:** Phrase questions so that "no" is the safe answer that moves you forward. "Is now a bad time to talk?" "Would it be a bad idea to…?" "Have you given up on this project?"
- **Why:** Saying "no" makes people feel safe and in control. Being pushed toward "yes" makes them defensive.
- **Use when:** Cold-call openings, asking for commitment, re-engaging a silent prospect. **Not when:** Used so often it feels like a trick. Deliberately mislabeling to provoke a "no" needs good rapport first.
- **Observable behavior:** Questions where "no" is the desired answer, especially at openings and asks.
- **Mistake → Better:** "Do you have 2 minutes?" → "Did I catch you at a bad time?" · "Are you still interested?" → "Have you given up on fixing the booking problem?"
- **Scenario:** A prospect went silent after receiving a proposal (Scenario S11).
- **Exercise:** Rewrite 10 yes-seeking questions as no-oriented ones.
- **Evaluation:** Opening and/or ask uses no-orientation. No "yes-ladder" sequences (3+ leading questions with obvious "yes" answers).
- **Source:** NSTD Ch.4. **Conflicts with HWF Part 3 Principle 5** (see §4).

#### NSTD-9 · Calibrated questions
- **Category:** Questioning · [F]
- **Description:** Open questions beginning with *What* or *How* (sometimes *Who*/*Which*). They ask the other side to help solve the problem and give them a sense of control. Avoid *Why*, which sounds accusatory.
- **Why:** They get people talking at length, uncover information, and make the counterpart work on your problem.
- **Use when:** Discovery, objections, after being attacked, to find people behind the table. **Not when:** You need a specific fact (just ask it), or they're too emotional to think (label first).
- **Observable behavior:** Ratio of How/What questions to closed questions. Calibrated question used *after* an objection rather than a counter-argument.
- **Mistake → Better:** "Why don't you want to switch?" → "What would have to be true for switching to make sense?"
- **Scenario:** "What's the biggest challenge you face with bookings?" "How does this decision get made on your side?"
- **Exercise:** Convert 15 closed or why-questions into calibrated ones. Then do a discovery roleplay with a limit of 2 closed questions.
- **Evaluation:** ≥60% of discovery questions are open/calibrated. ≤1 "why" aimed at the prospect. A calibrated question follows each major objection.
- **Source:** NSTD Ch.7; Appendix §IV (question bank).

#### NSTD-10 · "How am I supposed to do that?" and graduated ways to say no
- **Category:** Negotiation / protecting value · [F]
- **Description:** Turn down a demand by asking for help: "How am I supposed to do that?" Then escalate gently: "Your offer is very generous, I'm sorry, that just doesn't work for me" → "I'm sorry, I'm afraid I just can't do that" → "I'm sorry, no" → "No" (said calmly).
- **Why:** You refuse without hurting their pride, and they often improve their own offer.
- **Use when:** Discount demands, scope creep, low offers. **Not when:** The request is reasonable and cheap for you to grant. Then trade instead.
- **Observable behavior:** Responds to a demand with a How-question instead of an instant concession or argument.
- **Mistake → Better:** Client: "Can you add a blog for free?" "Sure…" → "I'd love to help. How am I supposed to do that and still hit the launch date we agreed?"
- **Scenario:** S15, client wants extra work for free.
- **Exercise:** The AI makes 4 escalating demands. The user must refuse 4 times without saying the word "no".
- **Evaluation:** Number of concessions without getting anything back (target 0). Uses at least 2 different refusal forms.
- **Source:** NSTD Ch.7, Ch.8 ("bid against themselves"), Ch.9.

#### NSTD-11 · Handling "fair"
- **Category:** Objection handling / negotiation · [F] + [R: ultimatum game, cited]
- **Description:** "Fair" is an emotional trigger. When someone says "We just want what's fair", don't concede. Invite them to show you where you've been unfair. Use "fair" positively yourself: "I want you to feel I'm treating you fairly. Stop me any time you feel I'm not."
- **Why:** An accusation of unfairness creates defensiveness that leads to irrational concessions.
- **Use when:** The counterpart invokes fairness. **Not when:** You really have treated them unfairly. Then admit it (HWF P3.3).
- **Observable behavior:** No concession within 2 turns of a "fair" accusation. An invitation to identify the unfairness.
- **Mistake → Better:** "OK, I'll knock 10% off" → "I apologize. Let's go back to where you felt I started treating you unfairly and fix it."
- **Scenario:** S14, client asking for a discount: "Other freelancers charge half, that's not fair."
- **Evaluation:** Concession size in turns after "fair" = 0. Response uses a label or an invitation.
- **Source:** NSTD Ch.6.

#### NSTD-12 · Loss framing and anchoring emotions
- **Category:** Persuasion (value) · [R: prospect theory, cited] + [F]
- **Description:** People take more risk to avoid a loss than to get an equal gain. Show what they lose by doing nothing. Before giving bad news, set emotional expectations low (an accusation audit).
- **Why:** Gains-only pitches feel optional. A clear cost of inaction creates real urgency.
- **Use when:** The status quo is costing them something real and measurable. **Not when:** The loss is invented or exaggerated. [GK] Fabricated urgency damages trust, and the product should penalize it.
- **Observable behavior:** Asks "What does doing nothing cost you?" and quantifies the loss in *the prospect's own* numbers.
- **Mistake → Better:** "Our system will boost bookings" → "You mentioned about 12 no-shows a week. What's that costing you each month?"
- **Scenario:** S03 discovery, S06 "I'll think about it".
- **Evaluation:** Cost of inaction comes from the prospect's own data. No fabricated scarcity or deadlines.
- **Source:** NSTD Ch.6; Appendix §IV.

#### NSTD-13 · Anchoring, ranges, who goes first, non-monetary terms
- **Category:** Negotiation (anchoring) · [R: anchor-and-adjust; Ames & Mason range study, cited] + [F]
- **Description:** In money negotiations, usually let the other side name a number first. If you have to go first, give a range whose *low end is your real target*, or mention what others charge. Then move to non-monetary terms: scope, timing, payment terms, case-study rights, referrals.
- **Why:** First numbers pull expectations toward them. Ranges seem less aggressive. Non-cash items can be cheap for one side and valuable to the other.
- **Use when:** Pricing discussions. **Not when:** You know the market well and the counterpart does too. Voss says opening quickly can be fine then.
- **Observable behavior:** Asks for their budget or expectation before naming a price. If they push, states a range or a reference price. Brings up at least one non-monetary variable.
- **Mistake → Better:** "It'll be $3,000" in the first minute → "Projects like this usually land between $4,500 and $6,000 depending on scope. What were you expecting to invest?"
- **Scenario:** S09 negotiating price.
- **Evaluation:** Order (who anchored first), presence of a range, number of non-monetary variables introduced.
- **Source:** NSTD Ch.6, Ch.9 ("Taking a punch").

#### NSTD-14 · Deadlines are mostly self-imposed
- **Category:** Negotiation / self-regulation · [F] + [A]
- **Description:** Deadlines push people into impulsive concessions. Most deadlines are flexible. "No deal is better than a bad deal."
- **Why:** Sellers who rush to close by end of month give away value.
- **Use when:** You feel pressure to concede to "close this week". **Not when:** It's a real contractual deadline. Test whether it is real.
- **Observable behavior:** Doesn't make extra concessions as the scenario timer or the AI's deadline approaches.
- **Mistake → Better:** "If you sign today I'll take 20% off" (self-imposed pressure) → ask what's driving *their* timing.
- **Evaluation:** Concessions in the last 20% of turns compared with earlier turns.
- **Source:** NSTD Ch.6.

#### NSTD-15 · Implementation: behind-the-table players and the Rule of Three
- **Category:** Discovery (decision process) / Closing · [F]
- **Description:** A "yes" means nothing without agreement on *how* it gets carried out. Find the people who aren't in the conversation but can kill the deal ("How does this affect the rest of your team?"). Confirm commitment three times in different forms: agreement, summary, a How-question about next steps.
- **Why:** Fake yeses and committees kill deals after the call ends.
- **Use when:** Any call that ends in agreement or with next steps. **Not when:** A trivial transactional sale.
- **Observable behavior:** Asks about other stakeholders. Confirms the next step with owner, date and action. Re-confirms commitment ≥2 more times.
- **Mistake → Better:** "Great, talk soon!" → "What happens on your side between now and Thursday? How will your partner weigh in?"
- **Scenario:** S10 asking for the sale, S13 upsell.
- **Evaluation:** Stakeholders identified. Concrete next step (who/what/when). Rule of Three achieved.
- **Source:** NSTD Ch.7 (Key Lessons), Ch.8.

#### NSTD-16 · Reading signals: tone, word incongruence, pronouns
- **Category:** Advanced listening · [R cited: Mehrabian] **[GK caveat]**
- **Description:** Notice when words don't match tone. Liars tend to use more words and more complex sentences ("Pinocchio effect"). Heavy use of "I/me" may mean the real decision maker is elsewhere, while "we/they" may mean a savvy decision maker. Use your own name to humanize yourself.
- **[GK] caveat:** "7-38-55" comes from Mehrabian's studies of *ambiguous emotional messages*. It is widely misapplied as "93% of all communication is nonverbal". The product should teach "notice mismatches between words and tone", **not** the percentages. The pronoun and lie heuristics are author claims. Treat them as prompts to ask more, never as conclusions.
- **Observable behavior:** Probes when a prospect's agreement sounds hesitant ("It sounds like there's a 'but' in there").
- **Evaluation:** User catches hesitation signals the AI was told to plant (e.g. "yeah, I guess that works").
- **Source:** NSTD Ch.8.

#### NSTD-17 · Counterpart styles: Analyst / Accommodator / Assertive
- **Category:** Adaptability / EQ · [F] + [R: lawyer-negotiator study, cited]
- **Description:** Analysts are methodical, want data, and use silence to think. Accommodators care about the relationship, like to chat, and may hide objections. Assertives believe time is money and need to be heard first. Treat people the way *they* need to be treated, not the way you would want to be.
- **Why:** The same tactic lands differently depending on style. Silence means "thinking" to an Analyst, "anger" to an Accommodator, and "your turn" to an Assertive.
- **Use when:** Adapting pace and content. **Not when:** Labeling the person rigidly. It's a hypothesis.
- **Observable behavior:** User adapts. Data and prep for Analysts, warmth plus probing for hidden objections with Accommodators, letting Assertives speak and then summarizing.
- **Product use:** **Every AI persona gets one of these three styles** (see `03-scenarios.md`).
- **Evaluation:** Style-appropriate moves (e.g. with an Assertive: summary + "that's right" before making the case).
- **Source:** NSTD Ch.9.

#### NSTD-18 · Ackerman bargaining
- **Category:** Negotiation (buyer side, or any haggle) · [F]
- **Description:** Set a target. Open at 65% of it. Move to 85%, 95%, then 100% in shrinking steps. Use empathy and indirect "no"s between moves so they counter first. End on a precise, non-round number plus a small non-monetary item to signal you're at your limit.
- **Why:** Shrinking moves signal you're running out of room. Precise numbers look calculated.
- **Use when:** The user is the *buyer* (procurement, vendor deals) or in an explicit haggle. **For sellers:** the mirror image applies. Concede in shrinking steps from your anchor and never in equal halves.
- **Not when:** Relationship deals where hard haggling would cost more than it gains. [GK] Also, a 35% gap at the opening can damage trust in small B2B relationships.
- **Observable behavior:** Concession sizes shrink. The final number is non-round. At least one "no" form between each move.
- **Mistake → Better:** Conceding in equal or growing amounts, or meeting in the middle → shrinking concessions, each one traded for something.
- **Evaluation:** Concession sequence is monotonically decreasing. Nothing given for free.
- **Source:** NSTD Ch.9.

#### NSTD-19 · Taking and returning a punch: no neediness, umbrage, "I" messages
- **Category:** Difficult conversations / negotiation · [F] + [R cited: anger studies]
- **Description:** When you get an extreme anchor, deflect ("What are we trying to accomplish here?") or move to terms. Set boundaries without anger: "I'm sorry, that doesn't work for me." Real, controlled anger aimed at the *proposal* can work. Fake anger backfires (author cites research). Don't be needy. Never treat the person as the enemy.
- **Use when:** Being lowballed or pressured. **Not when:** As an opening move. De-escalate first (Voss suggests a time-out).
- **Observable behavior:** Responds to aggression with a calibrated question or a calm boundary, not a counter-attack or immediate concession.
- **Mistake → Better:** "That's insulting!" or "OK…" → "I don't see how that would ever work. What are we trying to accomplish here?"
- **Evaluation:** No hostile language. No concession in the turn right after the punch.
- **Source:** NSTD Ch.9.

#### NSTD-20 · Black Swans and the three kinds of leverage
- **Category:** Advanced discovery · [F] + [A]
- **Description:** Every negotiation has unknown unknowns that change everything. Look for them through their worldview ("religion"), constraints, hidden desires and bad information. When someone seems irrational, assume they aren't. Leverage is positive (giving what they want), negative (what they could lose), or normative (their own standards).
- **Product use:** **Hidden information in scenarios is designed as a Black Swan.** The AI holds one fact that changes the right approach, and only deep discovery uncovers it.
- **Observable behavior:** Asks about constraints and context ("What's driving the timing?"). Explores apparent irrationality instead of dismissing it.
- **Evaluation:** Hidden "Black Swan" fact uncovered (yes/no). Turn number when it came out.
- **Source:** NSTD Ch.10.

#### NSTD-21 · The negotiation one sheet
- **Category:** Preparation · [F] + [R: goal-setting research, cited]
- **Description:** Five parts: (1) a specific, ambitious goal written down, (2) a short summary of the facts, (3) 3–5 labels for an accusation audit, (4) 3–5 calibrated questions, including ones about behind-the-table players and deal-killers, (5) non-cash items they could offer.
- **Why:** "You fall to your highest level of preparation" (the author's line). People who set specific, challenging goals get better deals.
- **Product use:** **Pre-call prep screen** in Phase 2. The coach then checks whether the user used what they prepared.
- **Source:** NSTD Appendix.

## 2. *How to Win Friends and Influence People* (Carnegie): HWF

### 2.1 Book-level analysis

| Dimension | Findings |
|---|---|
| **Core ideas** | People are driven by emotion and pride. Criticism makes them defensive. The deepest human desire is to feel important. You influence people by talking about what *they* want and by letting them reach conclusions themselves. |
| **Communication principles** | Be interested rather than interesting. Listen and encourage people to talk about themselves. Use names. Smile. Begin in a friendly way. |
| **Sales principles** (from examples) | Talk in terms of the buyer's wants, not yours (P1.3, P2.5). Let complaining customers talk themselves out (P3.6 "safety valve"). Make the idea the buyer's (P3.7). Dramatize ideas, i.e. demos and vivid proof (P3.11). |
| **Negotiation principles** | Avoid arguments, because you can't win one (P3.1). Respect their opinion and never say "you're wrong" (P3.2). Find common ground first (P3.5). Appeal to nobler motives (P3.10). |
| **Persuasion principles** | Arouse an eager want. Appeal to nobler motives. Dramatize. Throw down a challenge (P3.12). Give people a fine reputation to live up to (P4.7). |
| **Psychological principles** | Desire for importance and appreciation. Consistency pressure after saying "no" (P3.5, citing Overstreet). Saving face. |
| **Practical techniques** | Remember names. Sincere appreciation. Question instead of ordering. Talk about your own mistakes before criticizing. Point out mistakes indirectly. Praise every improvement. |
| **Common mistakes** | Criticizing, condemning, complaining. Winning arguments and losing the relationship. Talking about yourself and your wants. Flattery instead of appreciation. Ordering people around. |
| **Behaviors to develop** | Curiosity about others, sincere appreciation, admitting mistakes quickly, patience with complaints, empathy for the other's point of view. |
| **Behaviors to avoid** | Saying "you're wrong", arguing, self-centered talk, insincere praise, public criticism. |
| **Exercises (stated)** | "Nine Suggestions": read each chapter twice; stop to ask how you'll apply it; **review monthly; apply at every opportunity; make a game of it by paying a friend each time they catch you breaking a principle; weekly self-review of mistakes and lessons; keep a written record of applications.** This is the backbone of our learning loop. |
| **Real-world situations** | Sales calls, complaint handling, collections, managing staff, customer service, family. |

### 2.2 Concept cards

#### HWF-1 · Don't criticize, condemn or complain
- **Category:** Rapport / difficult conversations · [A]
- **Description:** Criticism puts people on the defensive and makes them justify themselves. It rarely changes behavior.
- **Use when:** Prospects badmouth their current vendor, or you're tempted to criticize a competitor or the client's past choice. **Not when:** Factual, requested feedback. Then use HWF-14.
- **Observable behavior:** No disparaging the prospect's current solution or decisions. Neutral framing ("What's working with your current setup? What isn't?").
- **Mistake → Better:** "Your site is terrible" → "It sounds like the site served you well early on. What's changed since then?"
- **Scenario:** S12, competitor comparison.
- **Evaluation:** Count of disparaging statements about the prospect, their choices or competitors (target 0).
- **Source:** HWF Part 1, Ch.1.

#### HWF-2 · Arouse an eager want / talk in terms of their interests
- **Category:** Persuasion (value communication) · [A]
- **Description:** People care about what *they* want. Frame every proposal around the other person's goals, in their language.
- **Why:** Feature lists about you don't motivate anyone. Their wants do.
- **Use when:** Any pitch, proposal or email. **Not when:** You haven't done discovery yet. Then you can't know their wants (combine with NSTD-9).
- **Observable behavior:** Ratio of "you/your" benefit statements to "we/our" feature statements. Value tied to things the prospect said earlier.
- **Mistake → Better:** "We use React and have 10 years' experience" → "You said Friday nights are when the phone line jams. This lets those orders come in online instead."
- **Exercise:** Rewrite a feature-heavy pitch so every sentence references a prospect goal.
- **Evaluation:** ≥70% of value statements reference something the prospect said.
- **Source:** HWF Part 1, Ch.3; Part 2, Ch.5. Parallel: Lowndes "You-Firstie" and "Bare the Buried WIIFM".

#### HWF-3 · Genuine interest and good listening
- **Category:** Communication fundamentals / rapport · [A]
- **Description:** Be truly interested in others. Encourage them to talk about themselves. Most people want to be heard more than they want a clever talker.
- **Use when:** Openings, discovery, relationship calls. **Not when:** Fake interest used as a script. People notice (Carnegie insists on sincerity).
- **Observable behavior:** Follow-up questions on personal or business details the prospect volunteered. No interrupting.
- **Mistake → Better:** Asking a question and then ignoring the answer → follow up on a detail ("You mentioned you opened a second location. How's that changed things?").
- **Evaluation:** At least 2 follow-ups that build on the prospect's previous answer.
- **Source:** HWF Part 2, Ch.1 & Ch.4.

#### HWF-4 · Remember and use names
- **Category:** Rapport · [A]
- **Description:** A person's name matters a lot to them. Remember it and use it.
- **Use when:** Greeting, key moments, follow-up. **Not when:** Overused. [GK] Repeating a name in every sentence feels manipulative. Lowndes's "Name Shower" is risky.
- **Observable behavior:** Uses the prospect's name 1–3 times over a call, especially at the opening and close.
- **Evaluation:** Name used 1–3 times. More than 1 use per 3 turns is flagged as overuse.
- **Source:** HWF Part 2, Ch.3. Related: Voss on using *your own* name (NSTD Ch.8); Lowndes "Name Shower" (HTTA Pt7).

#### HWF-5 · Make the other person feel important, sincerely, and give honest appreciation
- **Category:** Rapport / trust · [A]
- **Description:** Give honest, specific appreciation, not flattery. Recognize what people are proud of.
- **Use when:** Praise is earned and you can be specific. **Not when:** As a manipulation opener. Lowndes warns that insincere praise can wreck trust permanently.
- **Observable behavior:** Specific appreciation tied to facts ("Growing to two locations in three years is no small feat").
- **Mistake → Better:** "Love your restaurant!" (generic) → a specific, verifiable compliment.
- **Evaluation:** Every compliment is specific (references a fact from the conversation). Generic flattery counts against the score.
- **Source:** HWF Part 1, Ch.2; Part 2, Ch.6.

#### HWF-6 · You can't win an argument; never say "you're wrong"
- **Category:** Objection handling / difficult conversations · [A]
- **Description:** If you win an argument, the other person loses face and resents you. Show respect for their opinion. Disagree by asking questions and admitting you could be wrong.
- **Use when:** The prospect states something false or hostile. **Not when:** Safety or legal facts. Correct them, but gently.
- **Observable behavior:** Never says "you're wrong" or "that's not true". Uses "I may be wrong, but…" or a calibrated question instead.
- **Mistake → Better:** "No, that's not how SEO works" → "That's fair to question. What have you seen that makes you think SEO doesn't work for restaurants?"
- **Evaluation:** Direct contradictions = 0. Disagreement expressed through questions or a tentative frame.
- **Source:** HWF Part 3, Ch.1–2. Compare NSTD Ch.7 ("Don't try to force your opponent to admit that you are right").

#### HWF-7 · Admit mistakes quickly and emphatically
- **Category:** Trust / difficult conversations · [A]
- **Description:** When you're wrong, say so before they do, and do it fully. This disarms the other side.
- **Use when:** Angry customer (your fault), missed deadline. **Not when:** Admitting fault you don't have, which can have legal or contract consequences.
- **Observable behavior:** Early, specific ownership: "We missed the date. That's on us."
- **Evaluation:** If the scenario has seller fault, ownership appears within the first 2 user turns with no "but" deflection.
- **Source:** HWF Part 3, Ch.3. Close to the accusation audit (NSTD-5).

#### HWF-8 · Begin in a friendly way, start from shared ground ("yes, yes")
- **Category:** Rapport / framing · [A] + citation to Overstreet
- **Description:** Open warmly. Stress what you agree on and the shared goal. Get early agreement so the other person is moving forward rather than defending a "no".
- **⚠ Conflict:** Directly contradicts NSTD Ch.4 on *how*, though both agree on starting from shared goals. See §4.
- **Product stance:** Teach the *shared-goal framing* ("We both want the launch to go smoothly…"). **Don't** teach engineered yes-ladders ("Do you like saving money?"), which Voss explicitly criticizes and which the evaluator flags.
- **Source:** HWF Part 3, Ch.4–5.

#### HWF-9 · Let the other person talk, the "safety valve" for complaints
- **Category:** Difficult conversations (anger) · [A]
- **Description:** Let complaining people say everything without interruption. Respond only once they've finished.
- **Use when:** Angry customer, upset client. **Not when:** Abuse. Set a boundary (NSTD-19; HTTA "Broken Record").
- **Observable behavior:** First response to anger is listening (a short acknowledgment or a label), not explanation or defense.
- **Mistake → Better:** Interrupting to correct facts → "Tell me everything that happened." Then summarize.
- **Evaluation:** Number of turns before the user explains or defends (target: after a summary). No interrupting.
- **Source:** HWF Part 3, Ch.6.

#### HWF-10 · Let the other person feel the idea is theirs
- **Category:** Persuasion / closing · [A]
- **Description:** People believe ideas they reach themselves more than ideas handed to them. Ask questions and offer options so they arrive at the solution.
- **Use when:** Recommendations, upsells, change management. **Not when:** They explicitly ask for your expert recommendation. Then give it clearly.
- **Observable behavior:** Asks "What would you want to see happen?" before recommending. Builds the recommendation from the prospect's words.
- **Evaluation:** The recommendation reuses ≥2 phrases or goals the prospect stated.
- **Source:** HWF Part 3, Ch.7. **Agreement:** NSTD Ch.4/7 makes the same point ("persuasion is the other party convincing themselves").

#### HWF-11 · See things from their point of view; sympathize with their desires
- **Category:** Empathy · [A]
- **Description:** Ask yourself why they think what they think. Show understanding of their ideas and desires.
- **Observable behavior:** Statements that show you've taken their perspective, including on constraints like budget, their boss or risk.
- **Evaluation:** Explicitly acknowledges at least 1 prospect constraint before countering it.
- **Source:** HWF Part 3, Ch.8–9. **Agreement:** NSTD tactical empathy.

#### HWF-12 · Appeal to nobler motives, dramatize ideas, throw down a challenge
- **Category:** Persuasion (framing, storytelling) · [A]
- **Description:** Most people want to see themselves as fair and honorable, so appeal to that. Make ideas vivid with demonstrations and concrete images. Spark competitive drive ("I bet your team could…").
- **Use when:** Making the case, upsells, motivating action. **Not when:** A challenge to someone who is anxious may come across as pressure.
- **Observable behavior:** Uses a concrete story, example or visualization rather than abstract claims. Frames the decision around the prospect's values.
- **Evaluation:** At least 1 concrete example or story tied to the prospect's situation.
- **Source:** HWF Part 3, Ch.10–12.

#### HWF-13 · Ask questions instead of giving orders; let people save face
- **Category:** Leadership communication / client management · [A]
- **Description:** Suggest and ask rather than command. When someone is wrong, give them a way out that protects their dignity.
- **Use when:** Clients who missed their own deliverables, pushback from internal teams. **Not when:** Safety-critical instructions.
- **Observable behavior:** Requests phrased as questions. Face-saving lines ("Totally understandable given how busy launch week was").
- **Source:** HWF Part 4, Ch.4–5. Parallel: Lowndes "See No Bloopers".

#### HWF-14 · Giving feedback without resentment
- **Category:** Difficult conversations (feedback) · [A]
- **Description:** Start with sincere appreciation. Point out mistakes indirectly. Mention your own mistakes first. Make the fault seem easy to fix. Praise every improvement. Give the person a good reputation to live up to.
- **⚠ [GK] note:** Carnegie's original text links praise and criticism with "but". Many modern trainers warn that a "praise-but-criticism" sandwich makes people distrust the praise. The coach teaches separating them ("and" rather than "but", or praise given at another time).
- **Product use:** Also the **design spec for the AI coach's own feedback tone**. It starts with what the user did well, gives one fix, makes it look achievable, and points out improvements across attempts.
- **Source:** HWF Part 4, Ch.1–9.

#### HWF-15 · Carnegie's learning method
- **Category:** Learning system · [F]
- **Description:** A strong desire to improve. Ask yourself how you'll apply each idea. Apply at every opportunity. A weekly review of mistakes and lessons. A written log of applications with names, dates and results. Make it a game, with a friend who calls out your lapses.
- **Product use:** This is the core of the **learning loop** (`05-curriculum-30-day.md`): daily real-world challenge → reflection log → weekly review → streaks.
- **Source:** HWF "Nine Suggestions on How to Get the Most Out of This Book".

## 3. *How to Talk to Anyone* (Lowndes): HTTA-summary

> ⚠ Source is a third-party **summary**. Part numbers follow the summary's chapter list. Technique numbers (e.g. #10) are the summary's. Several sections are cut off. Treat every card here as **[F]/[A], provisional**. The "80% of first impression" and "85% of success" figures appear in the summary **without citation [GK: don't teach as fact]**.

### 3.1 Book-level analysis

| Dimension | Findings |
|---|---|
| **Core ideas** | Communication is many small learnable moves. First impressions form fast and come largely from body language and tone. Small talk is about making people comfortable, not about information. People like people who seem similar to them. |
| **Communication principles** | Match their mood (#10). Give answers that offer something to talk about, not bare one-liners. Listen for clues and follow up ("word detective"). Use their words, not yours. Prefer full empathy statements over "uh-huh". |
| **Sales principles** | Lead with the benefit when saying what you do. Learn insider vocabulary and the "hot button" issues of the prospect's industry. Make the "what's in it for you" explicit when asking for a meeting. On the phone, always ask about their timing. Systematic networking and follow-up ("tracking"). |
| **Negotiation principles** | Knowing insider language gets better prices ("Bluffing for Bargains", #43). Mostly absent otherwise. |
| **Persuasion principles** | Similarity (copying movement style, echoing), early "we", tailored analogies. Indirect or specific praise. |
| **Psychological principles** | Similarity-attraction ("proven", per summary, uncited). Overheard compliments feel more credible. Mental bookkeeping of favors ("scorecard"). |
| **Practical techniques** | ~92 techniques. Relevant subset in cards below. |
| **Common mistakes** | Bare answers. Asking "What do you do?" Generic smiles and compliments. Eating and drinking instead of mingling. Asking for a favor right after receiving one. Pointing out people's small blunders. |
| **Behaviors to develop** | Warm presence, curiosity, preparation (news, industry topics), follow-up systems, reviewing your own recorded calls. |
| **Behaviors to avoid** | Fidgeting, clichés, flattery, being self-focused. |
| **Exercises (stated/implied)** | Visualize before you meet people. "Scramble Therapy" (try new activities to broaden conversation range). **"Instant Replay": record important calls and listen again** (this directly supports the product's replay feature). Prepare a few benefit-based ways to describe your work. |
| **Real-world situations** | Networking events, phone prospecting, gatekeepers, industry events, client dinners. |

### 3.2 Concept cards

#### HTTA-1 · Presence: specific smile, eye contact, stillness
- **Category:** Confidence / first impressions · [F], uncited stats
- **Description:** A slower smile that seems personal to the one person (the "Flooding Smile"). Steady eye contact. Upright posture. Limit fidgeting for credibility.
- **Product relevance:** Only measurable in video mode, so Phase 3+. In voice mode, the proxy is a calm pace and few filler words ("um", "like").
- **[GK] caveat:** Eye-contact norms vary by culture. The summary itself flags this.
- **Source:** HTTA-summary Pt1.

#### HTTA-2 · Mood match
- **Category:** Rapport · [F]
- **Description:** Match the other person's emotional tone and energy before trying to move it.
- **Use when:** Openings, upset customers (calm seriousness, not cheerfulness). **Not when:** Matching anger or panic. Match *seriousness*, not hostility.
- **Observable behavior:** First response matches the register (an upset prospect gets a serious, calm reply, not "Great to hear from you!").
- **Evaluation:** Register mismatch in the first response is flagged.
- **Source:** HTTA-summary Pt2 (#10).

#### HTTA-3 · Never the naked answer; be a word detective
- **Category:** Conversation flow / clarity · [F]
- **Description:** Don't give one-word answers. Add a hook that gives the other person something to pick up. Listen for words that reveal interests and follow them.
- **Observable behavior:** Answers to the prospect's questions include a relevant hook. Follow-ups pick up specific words the prospect used.
- **Mistake → Better:** Prospect: "Do you do mobile sites?" "Yes." → "Yes. Actually most restaurant orders now start on phones. How many of yours come from mobile?"
- **Source:** HTTA-summary Pt2.

#### HTTA-4 · Benefit-first self-introduction and "How do you spend most of your time?"
- **Category:** Clarity / cold call opening · [F]
- **Description:** When asked what you do, give a short, relatable, benefit-focused answer. To learn about others without putting them on the spot, ask how they spend most of their time.
- **Observable behavior:** Opening line explains value in the prospect's terms within ~20 words.
- **Mistake → Better:** "I'm a full-stack developer" → "I help restaurants take more orders online without paying delivery-app commissions."
- **Exercise:** Write 3 benefit statements per target industry and test each in a cold-call roleplay.
- **Evaluation:** Self-description is benefit-led, ≤25 words, has no jargon.
- **Source:** HTTA-summary Pt3.

#### HTTA-5 · Insider preparation: industry lingo, hot buttons, trade reading
- **Category:** Credibility / preparation · [F]
- **Description:** Learn a few insider questions and current hot issues of the prospect's field. Outsider questions signal you don't belong.
- **Product use:** **Industry packs** (`03-scenarios.md` §3). Each includes insider vocabulary and hot-button issues. The evaluator rewards relevant insider questions.
- **Evaluation:** At least 1 industry-specific, informed question.
- **Source:** HTTA-summary Pt4 (#38–43).

#### HTTA-6 · Similarity techniques: echoing, potent imaging, empathizers, early "we"
- **Category:** Rapport · [F]
- **Description:** Repeat their specific words (echoing). Use analogies from their world. Use full empathy sentences rather than "uh-huh". Use inclusive "we" early.
- **Overlap:** Echoing ≈ Voss's mirroring, but the purpose differs: Lowndes = similarity, Voss = getting them to say more.
- **⚠ [GK] caution:** Copying body language and using "we" too early can feel manipulative if it's noticed. Rated only when it's natural and well timed.
- **Observable behavior:** Reuses the prospect's own terms (e.g. says "covers" with a restaurant owner instead of "seats").
- **Evaluation:** Uses ≥2 of the prospect's own key terms. Analogies come from the prospect's domain.
- **Source:** HTTA-summary Pt5 (#44–50).

#### HTTA-7 · Skilled praise, not flattery
- **Category:** Rapport / trust · [F]
- **Description:** Praise depends on sincerity, timing and how well you know the person. Indirect or relayed praise feels more credible. Always say *why* you're thanking someone.
- **Observable behavior:** Thank-you messages include the specific reason. Compliments are specific.
- **Evaluation:** Same as HWF-5. A "thank you" includes a reason in follow-up messages.
- **Source:** HTTA-summary Pt6, Pt3. **Partly conflicts with HWF Part 4 P6** ("lavish in your praise"). See §4.

#### HTTA-8 · Phone craft: ask about timing, translate gestures into words, replay your calls
- **Category:** Cold calling / phone · [F]
- **Description:** Always ask whether it's a good time. Since they can't see you, put your emotions into words. Recognize what's happening around them ("Sounds like a busy kitchen"). Record important calls and listen again to catch details you missed.
- **Observable behavior:** Asks about timing in the first 2 turns (combine with Voss: "Is now a bad time?"). Comments on background context.
- **Product use:** "Instant Replay" justifies the **transcript and audio replay with coach annotations** feature.
- **Source:** HTTA-summary Pt7 (#60–70).

#### HTTA-9 · Bad news with empathy; Broken Record for unwanted questions
- **Category:** Difficult conversations · [F]
- **Description:** Deliver bad news with attention to how the listener will feel. When someone keeps pushing on a topic you won't discuss, calmly repeat the same answer.
- **Use when:** Delays, price increases, scope refusals. Broken Record for repeated "just give me a discount".
- **Evaluation:** Bad news is preceded by acknowledging the impact. A repeated boundary stays consistent (doesn't erode over turns).
- **Source:** HTTA-summary Pt3.

#### HTTA-10 · Strategic networking and tracking
- **Category:** Prospecting / follow-up · [F]
- **Description:** Before an event, plan who's there, when to arrive, what to bring, the real purpose, the shared interests, and **how you'll follow up**. Approach people yourself ("be the chooser"). Record personal details and refer to them later ("tracking").
- **Product use:** Phase 3 "networking event" scenarios. **Tracking** → post-call "prospect notes" field.
- **Source:** HTTA-summary Pt8 (#71–77).

#### HTTA-11 · State the benefit (WIIFM), let them enjoy the favor, ignore small blunders
- **Category:** Asking / relationship etiquette · [F]
- **Description:** When asking for a meeting or a favor, make clear what's in it for them. Don't ask for a favor in return right after doing one. Ignore other people's small mistakes.
- **Observable behavior:** Meeting requests state the prospect's benefit. Upsell isn't pitched in the same breath as delivering a free favor.
- **Source:** HTTA-summary Pt9 (#78–82).

## 4. Where the books disagree (not hidden)

| # | Topic | NSTD (Voss) | HWF (Carnegie) | HTTA-summary (Lowndes) | Product resolution |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Seeking "yes"** | Beware "yes". Pushing for it makes people defensive. "No" makes them feel safe. Telemarketer yes-ladders are the example of what not to do (Ch.4). | Get the other person saying "yes, yes" right away. A "no" is hard to reverse because of pride (P3.5). | — | Teach Voss's *mechanics* (no-oriented asks, no yes-ladders) and Carnegie's *intent* (start from shared goals). The evaluator penalizes yes-ladders and rewards shared-goal framing. Both authors agree that the way someone's pride relates to their earlier answers matters. |
| 2 | **Compromise / win-win** | Never split the difference. Win-win thinking is often naive. "No deal is better than a bad deal" (Ch.6). But also: never create an enemy, and the person isn't the problem (Ch.9). | Avoid arguments. Goodwill matters most. Concede when you're wrong. | — | "Firm on value, warm on the person." Score concessions for *reciprocity* (did they get something back?) and score relationship tone separately. |
| 3 | **Conflict** | Conflict brings out truth and creativity. Embrace it (Ch.9). | The only way to win an argument is to avoid it (P3.1). | Big winners overlook small blunders. | Distinguish *disagreeing about the issue* (fine, needed) from *arguing with the person* (avoid). The rubric penalizes contradicting the person, not holding your position. |
| 4 | **Praise** | Barely uses praise. Uses labels to reinforce positive feelings. "Your offer is very generous" is a tactical courtesy. | Be hearty with approval and lavish with praise. Begin with praise (Part 4). | Unskilled praise can permanently damage trust. Prefer specific, indirect praise (Pt6). | Reward only **specific, factual** appreciation. Generic praise is neutral at best and penalized when it seems manipulative. |
| 5 | **Repeating their words** | Mirror 1–3 words to make them say more (information). | — | Echo their words and copy movements to feel similar (rapport). | Teach mirroring as the primary tool, with "use their vocabulary" as a secondary rapport behavior. Penalize obvious parroting. |
| 6 | **Using names** | Use *your own* name to humanize yourself ("forced empathy"). | Their name is the sweetest sound to them. | "Name Shower": use it often on the phone. | Use their name 1–3 times per call. Overuse is flagged [GK]. |
| 7 | **Who anchors first** | Usually let them go first, but extreme anchors are powerful, and the Ackerman model opens at 65%. Voss accepts both depending on information and experience (Ch.6, Ch.9). | — | Learn insider prices before buying. | Rule in the product: *information decides.* If you don't know their budget or the market, ask first. If you do, anchor with a range. |
| 8 | **Body-language statistics** | 7-38-55 (Ch.8). | — | 80% of the first impression is nonverbal. 85% of success is communication (uncited). | [GK] Don't teach the numbers. Teach "notice mismatches between words and tone, then ask about them". |
| 9 | **Logic vs emotion** | Emotion drives decisions. Logic-only negotiation is outdated (Ch.1, criticizing *Getting to Yes*). | Emotional and pride-driven too, but appeals to "nobler motives" (values). | Emotional comfort and similarity. | Consensus: **emotion first, logic to justify**. The rubric's "Persuasion" dimension needs both an emotional/values connection and concrete proof. |
| 10 | **BATNA** | Useful idea (credits Fisher & Ury), but fixating on it makes you aim low. Focus on an ambitious written goal (Appendix). | — | — | Prep screen asks for the *goal* first and the *walk-away* second, kept private. |

### Ethics note (product stance, not from the sources)
Some techniques can be used manipulatively: deliberate mislabeling to provoke a "no", using loss aversion, the "premature we", copying body language. Voss argues these simply reflect how people work (Ch.6 Key Lessons). Carnegie insists on sincerity throughout. **The product follows Carnegie on intent.** The evaluator penalizes invented urgency, false scarcity, invented social proof and deception, even when they "work" on the AI prospect.
